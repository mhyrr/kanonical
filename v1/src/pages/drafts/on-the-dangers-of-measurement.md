We live in a world awash in data.  This is a New Thing.  While the data has always existed, our ability to track, ingest, absorb, record and analyze all of this data is creating a revolution.  It's fair to consider this ability the Second Act of the Digital Age (the birth of computing being the First Act).

Several examples while reading Jesse's book on education

1st is education.  When you don't measure something for a long time, then measure it, what does that do and how does it change?  In the case of education, the nature of the measurement and what it measures changes the things it measures

Climate change is another, limited measurement over time in a grand scope.  Local minima/maxima.

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observer_effect_%28physics%29

usain bolt winning the 100m in 2013, vs. winning in 2009.  All business makes you slower.

The danger of believing that lot's of data allows you to optimize in ways you couldn't before.  Sometimes true, sometimes not.  Example: Theory X vs. Theory Y mgmt.  Because we have access to more fine-grained data on employees, we can "control" them more and make them more efficient.  Wrong!  My Mom's example of 34 pages of receipts and maps of driving.

The myth of uniformity, espeically in climate change.  Arctic vs. antarctic sea ice levels.  Forest levels over time.

Averaging large amounts of data.  Does it make sense?

There's an extension of confirmation bias in measurement - in that you automatically assume that whatever variable is measured is more important than others that aren't.  This is especially a problem when certain variables _can't_ be measured.  This doesn't make them unimportant.
